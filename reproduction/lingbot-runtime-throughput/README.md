Measured adaptive runs spend 4.5–5.2 seconds waiting for the player and 5.4–6.7 seconds rendering a chunk. This makes persistent workers and parallel episodes higher priority than another small attention improvement. No frame counts, solver steps, actor decision intervals, or episode budgets are reduced here.

The 171-case baseline/candidate pair has a full-budget ceiling of 42.53 warm GPU hours at conservative 6.0/7.3 seconds per rendered chunk and 4.8 seconds per actor call. Two warm GPUs need 21.3 hours; four 10.7 hours; eight 5.35 hours, before judging, retries, queues and review. Reloading every four episodes adds about 2.39 GPU hours. Four GPUs are the minimum practical deadline plan; eight provide substantially more room for failures and scoring. Pilot-duration extrapolation is much shorter (12.94 GPU hours) but four examples per category do not justify promising that estimate.

The existing FA4 kernel improves a stationary native H100 rollout by 1.16 times against the old stack. Actor waiting reduces the expected adaptive wall-time improvement to roughly 8–11 percent when adding the measured 4.8-second actor wait to the matched H100 kernel timings. Changing the Torch/kernel stack also changes generated pixels and needs task-quality validation before adoption.
